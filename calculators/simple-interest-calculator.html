Simple vs. Compound Interest Divergence Analysis

Examining the mathematical widening between linear and exponential interest functions across horizons.

1. Mathematical Model

Simple interest follows a linear polynomial: f(t) = P(1 + rt) with a constant first derivative: df/dt = Pr.

Compounding follows an exponential function: g(t) = P(1 + r/m)^(mt) whose derivative accelerates continuously with time.

2. The Taylor Expansion

Using continuous compounding e^(rt) = 1 + rt + (rt)^2/2! + (rt)^3/3! + ...

The delta between compound and simple interest equals P × [(rt)^2/2! + (rt)^3/3! + ...]. For small t, (rt)^2 is nearly zero, proving convergence over short durations.

3. Commercial Implications

Lenders prefer compounding because it extracts higher yields over long horizons. Borrowers benefit from simple interest loans (e.g. standard auto financing) because interest never compounds on unpaid principal.

Institutional Guide

Comprehensive Guide to Simple Interest Mathematics, Banker's Day-Count Conventions, and Commercial Debt Instruments

Simple interest represents the most fundamental mathematical foundation of credit and fixed-income contracts. Unlike compounding mechanisms that add accrued finance charges back into principal to generate exponential growth, simple interest is linear. The finance charge is strictly a function of the original principal amount, nominal annual interest rate, and elapsed duration. This guide examines the algebraic mechanics of simple interest, analyzes day-count conventions, investigates primary commercial applications, and contrasts linear growth with compound interest.

1. Algebraic Foundations and Mathematical Derivation

The simple interest formula expresses interest as an uninterrupted linear function. In continuous calculus terms, the rate of change of interest with respect to time remains invariant:

I = P × r × t

dI/dt = P × r = Constant Accrual Rate

The variables in this equation represent distinct economic parameters:

  • I (Interest Charge): The total dollar sum of finance charges generated across the contractual duration.
  • P (Principal): The original capital sum borrowed, financed, or placed on deposit. In pure simple interest, this principal balance never changes due to interest accrual.
  • r (Annual Interest Rate): The nominal percentage rate expressed as a decimal (e.g., 6.0% becomes 0.060).
  • t (Time Duration in Years): The elapsed loan term normalized into annual fractions.

To calculate the total accrued value (often designated as maturity value or future balance A), the interest charge is combined with the original principal:

A = P + I = P + (P × r × t) = P × (1 + r × t)

The term (1 + rt) functions as the linear accumulation factor. Because this factor is linear with respect to t, doubling the loan term precisely doubles the interest paid, maintaining complete proportionality throughout the life of the instrument.

2. Day-Count Conventions: The Banker's Rule (360-Day) vs. Exact Interest (365-Day)

When interest is contracted over a term measured in days rather than whole years, calculating t requires selecting a day-count convention. In commercial and statutory finance, two primary conventions dictate the annual fraction denominator:

Ordinary Interest (The Banker's Rule: Actual/360)

Under the 360-day rule, time is defined as:

t = Number of Days / 360

Originating in 19th-century commercial banking before the advent of mechanical calculators, the 360-day year divided the annual cycle into twelve equal 30-day months. Although created for manual bookkeeping convenience, this convention introduces an asymmetry that favors the creditor.

Exact Interest (Actual/365)

Under exact interest conventions, time is defined as:

t = Number of Days / 365 (or 366 in leap year)

Exact interest reflects the physical solar calendar. It is standard in United States Treasury notes, municipal bond coupons, consumer installment loans, and consumer checking accounts.

The Mathematical Premium of the Banker's Rule

Historical analyses documented by the Mathematical Association of America (MAA) confirm that applying the 360-day denominator to an actual day count (the "Actual/360" or Banker's method) systematically increases finance charges. Comparing the two formulas for identical principal and interest rates demonstrates the mathematical multiplier:

I_ordinary / I_exact = (P × r × (Days / 360)) / (P × r × (Days / 365)) = 365 / 360 ≈ 1.0138889

Consequently, ordinary interest produces exactly 1.3889% more interest income than exact interest. On a $100,000,000 corporate credit facility with an 8.0% interest rate, the Banker's Rule extracts an additional $111,111 in annual finance charges compared to an exact 365-day schedule. Because of this structural yield boost, commercial money markets worldwide routinely price unsecured interbank deposits and corporate commercial paper using Actual/360.

3. Commercial Instruments Governed by Simple Interest

While retail deposit accounts and consumer credit cards use compound interest, simple interest remains the governing standard across critical debt and capital market sectors:

A. Promissory Notes and Commercial Paper

Under Article 3 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), a promissory note represents an unconditional written promise by one party to pay a fixed sum of money to another party either on demand or at a definite determinable future time. Promissory notes between business entities, bridge loans, and shareholder financing notes universally specify simple interest to prevent disputes regarding compounding intervals.

Commercial paper—unsecured, short-term corporate debt issued to finance payroll and accounts receivable with maturities capped at 270 days under SEC exemption rules—is traded directly on simple interest discount and coupon yields. The Federal Reserve Board tracks and publishes commercial paper interest rate indexes through its daily H.15 statistical release.

B. United States Treasury Bills (T-Bills)

U.S. Treasury Bills are short-term government debt obligations with maturities of 4, 8, 13, 17, 26, or 52 weeks. T-Bills do not pay regular periodic coupons; rather, they are issued at a discount to face value and mature at par. The discount yield is determined using simple interest on a 360-day ordinary year:

Bank Discount Yield = ((Face Value - Purchase Price) / Face Value) × (360 / Days to Maturity)

To convert this bank discount rate to an investment yield comparable with standard coupon-bearing notes, analysts calculate the bond equivalent yield (BEY) using a 365-day denominator against the purchase price rather than par.

C. Statutory Pre-Judgment and Post-Judgment Interest

In litigation across federal and state jurisdictions, courts award pre-judgment and post-judgment interest to compensate successful plaintiffs for the loss of capital use during trial proceedings. Under 28 U.S. Code § 1961, federal judgments accrue interest pegged to the weekly average 1-year constant maturity Treasury yield, calculated on a simple interest basis from the date of judgment entry until final settlement.

4. The Mathematical Divergence: Linear vs. Exponential Expansion

The divergence between simple and compound interest represents the divide between arithmetic progression and geometric compounding. Over brief terms, the two curves trace nearly identical paths. Over multi-year horizons, the compounding curve accelerates upward away from the simple interest line:

This divergence demonstrates why consumers carrying revolving debt on credit cards suffer under daily compounding interest, whereas borrowers with simple interest fixed installment loans know that every dollar paid directly reduces their legal obligation.

5. Federal Lending Disclosures: The Truth in Lending Act and Regulation Z

Enacted by Congress to safeguard consumers against obscure borrowing costs, the Truth in Lending Act (TILA), implemented through Regulation Z (12 CFR Part 1026) and supervised by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), enforces rigorous disclosure standards for simple interest financing.

Nominal Rate vs. Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

In a simple interest agreement without fees, the nominal interest rate equals the APR. However, if a lender charges upfront document preparation fees, application fees, or appraisal charges, Regulation Z mandates that these prepaid finance charges be amortized into the legal APR.

Under TILA, advertising solely the simple interest rate while concealing upfront finance surcharges constitutes an illegal deceptive credit practice.

The 360-Day Disclosure Requirement

When financial institutions apply the 360-day Banker's Rule to consumer credit transactions, federal courts have ruled that failing to disclose the 360-day year computation violates Regulation Z if the effective annual interest extracted exceeds the advertised APR.

Consequently, consumer lenders must either state the exact 365-day basis or adjust their advertised APR upward by a factor of 365/360.

6. Practical Computational Case Studies

The following verified numerical examples demonstrate how simple interest calculations operate in real-world commercial situations:

Case 1: 90-Day Promissory Note Under Banker's Rule

A wholesale distributor issues a $25,000 promissory note to a supplier with a 90-day maturity at 7.20% annual simple interest using ordinary 360-day interest:

  • Time t = 90 / 360 = 0.2500 years
  • Interest I = $25,000 × 0.0720 × 0.2500 = $450.00
  • Maturity Payment A = $25,000 + $450.00 = $25,450.00
  • Daily Accrual = ($25,000 × 0.0720) / 360 = $5.00 / day

Case 2: Auto Title Loan With Monthly Accrual

A consumer takes a $2,500 short-term title loan for 6 months at 24.0% annual interest on an exact 365-day basis:

  • Time t = 6 / 12 = 0.5000 years (or 182.5 / 365)
  • Interest I = $2,500 × 0.2400 × 0.5000 = $300.00
  • Total Payoff A = $2,500 + $300.00 = $2,800.00
  • Monthly Simple Interest = $300.00 / 6 = $50.00 / month

The simple interest formula is I = P × r × t, where I is the interest earned, P is the initial principal, r is the annual interest rate expressed in decimal form, and t is the time horizon normalized to years. When time is stated in months, t = months / 12. When stated in days, t = days / 365 for exact interest, or t = days / 360 for ordinary interest under the Banker's Rule.

The Banker's Rule uses a 360-day commercial year divided into twelve 30-day months. Because the denominator is 360 instead of 365, the fraction of the year (Days / 360) is larger than (Days / 365). This arithmetic shortcut increases the interest yield by exactly 365/360 or 1.01389 (a 1.39% increase in total interest) in favor of the lending bank. Commercial banks still use this rule today for pricing corporate credit lines and money market paper.

Simple interest is linear: interest accrues only on the initial principal, so the same dollar amount of interest is added each day. Compound interest is exponential: accrued interest is added to the principal balance at the end of each compounding cycle, meaning future interest is charged on top of previous interest. For terms under 90 days, the divergence between the two is small, but over multiple years, compounding rapidly outpaces simple interest.
