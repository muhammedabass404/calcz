Mathematical Derivation of Radioactive & Pharmacokinetic Decay

Rigorous calculus proof from differential rate equations to integrated exponential models and logarithmic transforms.

1 The Differential Rate Law of Radioactive Disintegration

In a large population of unstable radioactive nuclei or drug molecules undergoing first-order elimination, the instantaneous rate of decay $\frac{dN}{dt}$ is directly proportional to the total number of undecayed particles $N$ present at that exact instant. The proportionality constant is the decay constant $\lambda$ (with physical units of inverse time $\text{s}^{-1}$, $\text{hr}^{-1}$, or $\text{yr}^{-1}$):

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N$$

The negative sign denotes that the quantity $N$ decreases continuously as time $t$ progresses forward.

2 Separation of Variables & Definite Integration

Separating variables by dividing both sides by $N$ and multiplying by $dt$ gives:

$$\frac{1}{N} \, dN = -\lambda \, dt$$

Integrating both sides from initial boundary conditions at $t = 0$ (where $N = N_0$) to elapsed time $t$ (where $N = N(t)$):

$$\int_{N_0}^{N(t)} \frac{1}{N} \, dN = -\lambda \int_{0}^{t} dt$$
$$\left[ \ln(N) \right]_{N_0}^{N(t)} = -\lambda t \implies \ln(N(t)) - \ln(N_0) = -\lambda t \implies \ln\left(\frac{N(t)}{N_0}\right) = -\lambda t$$

Exponentiating both sides with Euler's base $e$:

$$N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t}$$

3 Derivation of the Half-Life Formula ($t_{1/2}$)

By definition, half-life $t_{1/2}$ is the elapsed time at which exactly half of the initial quantity remains, meaning $N(t_{1/2}) = \frac{N_0}{2}$. Substituting this into the exponential solution:

$$\frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} \implies \frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}}$$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

$$\ln\left(\frac{1}{2} \right) = -\lambda t_{1/2} \implies -\ln(2) = -\lambda t_{1/2} \implies \ln(2) = \lambda t_{1/2}$$

Solving for $t_{1/2}$ and $\lambda$ respectively yields the fundamental equivalence:

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.69314718}{\lambda} \quad \iff \quad \lambda = \frac{\ln(2)}{t_{1/2}} \approx \frac{0.69314718}{t_{1/2}}$$

Rewriting in base-2 reveals the intuitive doubling-division formulation:

$$N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t} = N_0 \, e^{-\left(\frac{\ln(2)}{t_{1/2}}\right) t} = N_0 \left( e^{\ln(2)} \right)^{-\frac{t}{t_{1/2}}} = N_0 \, 2^{-\frac{t}{t_{1/2}}} = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2} \right)^{\frac{t}{t_{1/2}}}$$

4 Derivation of Mean Lifetime ($\tau$)

While individual atoms decay stochastically, the expectation value or arithmetic average lifespan $\tau$ of an unstable nucleus before disintegration is given by integrating over all time from $0$ to $\infty$:

$$\tau = \langle t \rangle = \frac{1}{N_0} \int_{0}^{\infty} t \left(-\frac{dN}{dt} \right) dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} \lambda t \, e^{-\lambda t} dt$$

Using integration by parts with $u = t$ and $dv = \lambda e^{-\lambda t} dt$:

$$\tau = \left[ -t e^{-\lambda t} \right]_{0}^{\infty} + \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-\lambda t} dt = 0 + \left[ -\frac{1}{\lambda} e^{-\lambda t} \right]_{0}^{\infty} = \frac{1}{\lambda}$$
$$\tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln(2)} \approx 1.442695 \times t_{1/2}$$

During one mean lifetime $\tau$, the sample decays to $e^{-1} \approx 36.7879\%$ of its initial value.

5 Pharmacokinetic One-Compartment Clearance Equivalence

In clinical pharmacology, drug clearance follows the identical mathematical architecture. After an intravenous bolus injection into an apparent volume of distribution $V_d$, systemic clearance $CL$ (volume of blood cleared per unit time) defines the elimination rate constant $k_e$:

$$k_e = \frac{CL}{V_d} \quad \implies \quad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{k_e} = \frac{0.693 \times V_d}{CL}$$

The plasma concentration $C_p(t)$ at any post-distribution time $t$ obeys:

$$C_p(t) = C_0 \, e^{-k_e t} = \left(\frac{\text{Dose}}{V_d} \right) e^{-\left(\frac{CL}{V_d} \right) t}$$

Scientific Reference & Physical Principles

Nuclear Decay Mechanics, First-Order Kinetics, and Radiocarbon Geochronology

An authoritative technical examination of stochastic quantum tunneling, exponential decay invariants, Willard Libby's radiocarbon dating methodology, and pharmacological drug clearance.

1. Quantum Foundations and the Stochastic Nature of Nuclear Decay

Radioactive decay represents an inherently stochastic quantum mechanical phenomenon. An unstable nucleus—possessing an unfavorable ratio of protons to neutrons, or residing in an excited nuclear isomeric state—transitions to a configuration of lower potential energy through the emission of ionizing particles or electromagnetic radiation. These emissions encompass alpha particles ($^4_2\text{He}$ nuclei), beta-minus electrons ($\beta^-$) accompanied by electron antineutrinos, beta-plus positrons ($\beta^+$) accompanied by electron neutrinos, orbital electron capture, or isometric gamma ($\gamma$) photons.

Under the quantum tunneling framework first formulated by George Gamow in 1928, an alpha particle confined within a nucleus encounters a finite nuclear potential barrier established by the strong nuclear force. The wave function of the alpha particle possesses a non-zero probability amplitude extending through the Coulomb barrier. Each collision with the barrier represents an independent trial with a fixed probability of transmission per second. Because this barrier penetration probability is constant for any specific nuclide, the decay of an individual nucleus is completely random and independent of its age.

Consequently, an atomic nucleus that has persisted for 10 billion years has the exact same probability of decaying in the next second as an identical nucleus synthesized a microsecond ago. This memoryless property defines radioactive decay as a pure Poisson process. When aggregated across astronomical populations ($>10^{20}$ atoms), macroscopic decay strictly converges to a continuous, deterministic exponential curve governed by the decay constant $\lambda$.

Measurements published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Physical Measurement Laboratory confirm that nuclear decay rates remain extraordinarily invariant under terrestrial temperature, pressure, gravitational fields, and chemical hybridization. The nuclear force operates at distances on the order of femtometers ($10^{-15}\text{ m}$), whereas chemical bonding and thermal molecular agitation operate at angstrom scales ($10^{-10}\text{ m}$). Outer valence electron configurations exert negligible perturbation on nuclear barrier potentials, establishing radioactive half-lives as reliable cosmic chronometers.

2. First-Order Reaction Kinetics and the Differential Rate Law

In chemical and nuclear kinetics, radioactive decay represents a prototypical unimolecular, first-order reaction. The instantaneous activity $A(t)$, defined as the number of disintegrations occurring per unit time, is directly proportional to the total population of parent nuclei $N(t)$:

$$A(t) = -\frac{dN(t)}{dt} = \lambda N(t)$$

The International System of Units (SI) measures radioactivity in Becquerels (Bq), where $1\text{ Bq} = 1\text{ disintegration per second}$. The historical unit, the Curie (Ci), originally calibrated against the radioactivity of one gram of pure Radium-226 by Marie Curie, is defined exactly as $1\text{ Ci} = 3.7 \times 10^{10}\text{ Bq} = 37\text{ GBq}$.

Integrating this differential rate law establishes the exponential decay function:

$$N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2} \right)^{\frac{t}{t_{1/2}}}$$

Three interrelated time constants characterize this first-order system:

  • Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$): The duration required for the population to decline by half: $t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693147}{\lambda}$.
  • Mean Lifetime ($\tau$): The arithmetic expectation value of survival time for a particle: $\tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln(2)} \approx 1.442695 \times t_{1/2}$. In one mean lifetime, the population diminishes to $1/e \approx 36.7879\%$ of its initial quantity.
  • Decay Constant ($\lambda$): The instantaneous fractional rate of loss per unit time: $\lambda = \frac{\ln(2)}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{\tau}$.

3. Radiocarbon Dating Principles and Willard Libby's Breakthrough

Developed in 1949 by Willard F. Libby at the University of Chicago—an achievement recognized with the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry—radiocarbon dating revolutionized archaeology, paleoanthropology, and Quaternary geology.

The mechanism originates in the upper atmosphere. Energetic cosmic rays collide with atmospheric gas molecules, generating thermalized secondary neutrons ($n$). When a slow neutron strikes a stable Nitrogen-14 nucleus ($^{14}_7\text{N}$), an $(n, p)$ transmutation reaction occurs:

$$^{14}_7\text{N} + \,^1_0 n \longrightarrow \,^{14}_6\text{C} + \,^1_1 p$$

The newly synthesized Carbon-14 is oxidized into radioactive carbon dioxide ($^{14}\text{CO}_2$). Atmospheric mixing rapidly disperses this tracer across the troposphere, where autotrophic photosynthetic plants assimilate it into organic carbohydrates. Herbivores and apex carnivores subsequently incorporate Carbon-14 throughout the trophic food web.

Because respiration and feeding continuously exchange carbon with the biosphere, every living organism maintains a dynamic steady-state equilibrium ratio of Carbon-14 to stable Carbon-12 ($^{14}\text{C}/^{12}\text{C} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-12}$, corresponding to approximately $13.56\text{ disintegrations per minute per gram of carbon}$).

Upon biological death, metabolic exchange ceases immediately. No new Carbon-14 enters the tissues. The trapped $^{14}_6\text{C}$ atoms decay back to stable $^{14}_7\text{N}$ via beta-minus emission with a standardized physical half-life of $5,730 \pm 40\text{ years}$ (Cambridge half-life adopted by the International Atomic Energy Agency):

$$^{14}_6\text{C} \longrightarrow \,^{14}_7\text{N} + \beta^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$

By quantifying the residual specific activity or directly counting isotopic ratios using Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS), researchers calculate the elapsed post-mortem time $t$:

$$t = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln(2)} \ln\left( \frac{A_0}{A(t)} \right) = \frac{5,730}{0.693147} \ln\left( \frac{A_0}{A(t)} \right)$$

Because background radiation and instrument detection thresholds constrain measurement of samples containing less than $0.1\%$ of modern activity, radiocarbon dating reliably reaches up to roughly 10 half-lives, establishing an effective diagnostic horizon of approximately 50,000 to 57,000 years before present. Raw radiocarbon ages must also be calibrated against dendrochronological tree-ring sequences and marine coral archives (such as the IntCal20 calibration dataset) to correct for historical fluctuations in cosmic ray flux and geomagnetic field intensity.

4. Realistic Worked Numerical Scenarios

Case Study A: Radiocarbon Dating of an Ancient Wooden Artifact

An archaeological excavation uncovers an ancient cedar timber. Mass spectrometry reveals that the wood contains $23.50\%$ of the $^{14}\text{C}$ concentration observed in modern atmospheric wood. How old is the artifact?

$N(t) / N_0 = 0.2350$
$t_{1/2} = 5,730\text{ years}$
$\lambda = \ln(2) / 5,730 \approx 1.20968 \times 10^{-4}\text{ yr}^{-1}$
$t = \frac{\ln(1 / 0.2350)}{\lambda} = \frac{\ln(4.2553)}{1.20968 \times 10^{-4}} = \frac{1.44817}{1.20968 \times 10^{-4}} \approx \mathbf{11,971.5\text{ years}}$

The sample dates to roughly 11,972 years before present, placing it during the Younger Dryas terminal Pleistocene epoch.

Case Study B: Technetium-99m Diagnostic Radiopharmaceutical Dose Decay

A hospital radiopharmacy prepares a $740.0\text{ MBq}$ ($20.0\text{ mCi}$) dose of Technetium-99m ($t_{1/2} = 6.0067\text{ hours}$) at 07:00 AM for a myocardial perfusion scan scheduled for 01:00 PM (6 hours later). What activity remains at injection?

$N_0 = 740.0\text{ MBq}, \quad t = 6.0\text{ hours}, \quad t_{1/2} = 6.0067\text{ hours}$
$n = 6.0 / 6.0067 = 0.99888\text{ half-lives}$
$N(t) = 740.0 \times (0.5)^{0.99888} \approx \mathbf{370.29\text{ MBq}}\text{ (or } 10.01\text{ mCi)}$

Over the 6-hour interval, exactly 49.96% of the initial metastable nuclide decays into ground-state Technetium-99 via 140.5 keV gamma emission.

Case Study C: Caffeine Plasma Clearance Following Espresso Ingestion

An adult drinks a double espresso containing $200.0\text{ mg}$ of caffeine. Assuming instantaneous absorption and a typical biological elimination half-life of $5.0\text{ hours}$, how much active drug remains in the bloodstream 15 hours later?

$N_0 = 200.0\text{ mg}, \quad t = 15.0\text{ hours}, \quad t_{1/2} = 5.0\text{ hours}$
$n = 15.0 / 5.0 = 3.0\text{ half-lives}$
$N(15) = 200.0 \times (0.5)^3 = 200.0 \times 0.125 = \mathbf{25.00\text{ mg}}$

After 3 half-lives, 87.5% has been metabolized via hepatic CYP1A2 demethylation into paraxanthine, theobromine, and theophylline, leaving 25 mg active.

5. Biological vs. Elimination Half-Life in Pharmacology

In clinical medicine and pharmacology, understanding the distinction between elimination half-life, biological half-life, and effective half-life is fundamental for therapeutic dosage design:

Elimination Half-Life ($t_{1/2\beta}$)

Quantifies the time needed to reduce circulating plasma drug concentration by 50% during the terminal linear elimination phase. It is governed mechanically by systemic clearance ($CL$, mediated by hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes and renal glomerular filtration) and apparent volume of distribution ($V_d$):

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693 \times V_d}{CL}$$

Biological Half-Life ($t_{1/2\text{ bio}}$)

Quantifies the duration required for the drug's physiological effect to drop by half. Biological half-life can deviate substantially from plasma concentration when compounds form irreversible covalent receptor bonds (e.g., aspirin inhibiting cyclooxygenase-1 in platelets) or generate active metabolites with prolonged residence times.

In nuclear medicine, when administering radiopharmaceuticals (such as Iodine-131 for thyroid carcinomas), clinicians compute the Effective Half-Life ($t_{1/2\text{ eff}}$), combining physical radiological disintegration ($t_p$) with biological excretory clearance ($t_b$):

$$\frac{1}{t_{1/2\text{ eff}}} = \frac{1}{t_p} + \frac{1}{t_b} \quad \implies \quad t_{1/2\text{ eff}} = \frac{t_p \times t_b}{t_p + t_b}$$

Because both mechanisms proceed in parallel, the effective half-life is always shorter than either individual component, protecting patients against protracted ionizing radiation exposure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Detailed scientific and practical clarifications regarding decay calculations and half-life mechanics.

Half-life (symbol $t_{1/2}$) is the time required for exactly one-half of the unstable atomic nuclei or active drug molecules in a given sample to undergo radioactive disintegration or pharmacological elimination. It represents a statistical characteristic of first-order exponential decay governed by the equation $N(t) = N_0 (1/2)^{t / t_{1/2}}$.
The decay constant ($\lambda$) quantifies the instantaneous probability of decay per unit time and is related to half-life by $\lambda = \frac{\ln(2)}{t_{1/2}} \approx \frac{0.693147}{t_{1/2}}$. The mean lifetime ($\tau$) represents the average lifespan of an unstable particle before disintegration, calculated as $\tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln(2)} \approx 1.442695 \times t_{1/2}$.
Radiocarbon dating measures the residual ratio of radioactive Carbon-14 ($t_{1/2} = 5,730\text{ years}$) to stable Carbon-12 in organic remains. While alive, organisms maintain an equilibrium ratio through atmospheric exchange and feeding. At death, uptake ceases and Carbon-14 decays through beta emission. Solving the decay formula $t = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln(2)} \ln\left(\frac{N_0}{N(t)}\right)$ yields the chronological age of the sample, which is calibrated against dendrochronological IntCal standards.
Elimination half-life ($t_{1/2\beta}$) refers to the time needed to reduce the plasma drug concentration by 50% during the terminal elimination phase, dictated by clearance ($CL$) and volume of distribution ($V_d$) through $t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693 \times V_d}{CL}$. Biological half-life refers to the duration required for the observed physiological or pharmacological response to diminish by half, which may outlast plasma presence due to receptor binding affinity or active metabolites.
In pharmacokinetics, after 1 half-life 50% remains; after 2 half-lives 25% remains; after 3 half-lives 12.5% remains; after 4 half-lives 6.25% remains (93.75% eliminated); and after 5 half-lives only 3.125% remains (96.875% eliminated). Clinically, a drug is considered completely cleared from the system after 5 half-lives. Conversely, during regular repeated dosing, plasma accumulation achieves steady-state equilibrium at 4 to 5 half-lives.
Radioactive decay rates for alpha, beta, and gamma emissions originate within the nuclear force structure of the atomic nucleus, shielded by electron shells. Environmental variations such as extreme heat, freezing, planetary pressures, or chemical reactions do not alter nuclear half-life. Minor exceptions occur only in specialized electron-capture isotopes (such as Beryllium-7) under extreme chemical compression, with shifts bounded under 1%.
Divide the elapsed time ($t$) by the half-life ($t_{1/2}$) to find the number of elapsed half-lives $n = t / t_{1/2}$. The remaining percentage is calculated as $P = 100\% \times (0.5)^n = 100\% \times e^{-\lambda t}$. For example, after 3 half-lives, $P = 100\% \times (0.5)^3 = 12.5\%$.
Radioisotope half-lives and decay constants are standardized by international metrology organizations including the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Physical Measurement Laboratory and the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Nuclear Data Section via Evaluated Nuclear Structure Data Files (ENSDF).
