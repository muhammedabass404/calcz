Comprehensive Guide to Mortgage Recasting

When borrowers come into a large sum of money—whether through an inheritance, selling a secondary property, or receiving a substantial bonus—many wonder how best to apply it toward their mortgage. While making a standard extra payment reduces your principal and shortens your loan payoff time, it does not change your required monthly payment. This is where mortgage recasting becomes a powerful financial tool.

What is a Mortgage Recast vs Refinancing?

Mortgage recasting (also known as loan re-amortization) occurs when you pay a significant lump sum toward your loan's principal balance and then ask your lender to recalculate your monthly payments based on the new, lower balance. The essential benefit is that your loan term and your interest rate remain exactly the same.

In contrast, refinancing involves replacing your existing loan with a completely new loan. Refinancing requires full underwriting, an appraisal, and closing costs that often range from 2% to 5% of the loan amount. If you already have a low fixed interest rate, refinancing in a higher-rate environment is a poor financial decision. Recasting allows you to keep that favorable low rate while immediately reducing your monthly cash flow obligations.

Requirements and Lender Guidelines

Not all mortgages are eligible for recasting, and lenders impose specific rules. Under Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines (which govern most conventional loans in the US), lenders generally permit recasting, though it is usually at their discretion. Common requirements include:

  • Minimum Lump Sum: Most lenders require a minimum principal reduction of $5,000 to $10,000. Some lenders calculate this as a percentage, such as 10% of the remaining balance.
  • Good Standing: The borrower must have a history of on-time payments and cannot be in forbearance or default.
  • Loan Type: Conventional loans are typically eligible. Jumbo loans may also qualify depending on the portfolio lender.
  • Administrative Fees: Lenders typically charge a recast processing fee ranging from $150 to $500.

Important Note on Government Loans: Loans backed by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) generally do not allow for recasting. If you hold an FHA or VA loan, you must typically refinance to alter your monthly payment structure.

A Numerical Scenario

To understand the mathematics behind a recast, consider a borrower with a remaining loan balance of $300,000, an interest rate of 6.5%, and 25 years remaining on their 30-year fixed-rate mortgage. Their current monthly payment for principal and interest is approximately $2,025.

If this borrower inherits $50,000 and applies it as a lump sum recast payment, the new principal balance drops to $250,000. The lender recalculates the loan amortized over the same 25-year remaining term at the same 6.5% interest rate. The new monthly payment becomes approximately $1,687.

The Results: By recasting, the borrower saves $338 per month. Over the remaining 25 years (300 months), the total amount of interest paid is drastically reduced. While the upfront cost was the $50,000 lump sum plus a nominal recast fee (e.g., $250), the long-term interest savings amount to tens of thousands of dollars.

When Does Recasting Make Sense?

Recasting is an excellent strategy for individuals who want to lower their monthly expenses to free up cash flow for other investments, retirement savings, or living expenses. It is particularly popular among homeowners who purchase a new home before selling their old one. Once the old home sells, the proceeds can be applied to the new mortgage as a recast, aligning the monthly payment with what it would have been had they put a large down payment initially.

However, if you are looking to pay off your mortgage faster, simply making standard extra principal payments without recasting will accelerate your payoff date faster than recasting, though your required monthly payment will not drop until the loan is fully satisfied.
