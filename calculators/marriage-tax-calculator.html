Understanding the Marriage Tax Penalty and Marriage Bonus in 2026

The United States federal tax code has grappled with the mathematical tension between progressive tax rates, equal taxation of married couples with equal incomes, and marriage neutrality for over a century. Under a progressive income tax system, achieving all three goals simultaneously is mathematically impossible. When two individuals marry, their combined household income alters their tax bracket boundaries, standard deductions, and phaseout thresholds, resulting in either a marriage bonus (a lower joint tax liability) or a marriage tax penalty (a higher joint tax liability).

The Structural Trilemma: Why the Tax Code Cannot Be Marriage Neutral

As documented in comprehensive analyses by the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) and the Tax Foundation, tax economists describe the taxation of couples through the concept of the "tax trilemma." A progressive income tax system can possess, at most, two of the following three core principles:

  1. Progressive Taxation: Individuals with higher taxable income pay higher marginal and effective rates than lower earners.
  2. Equal Taxation of Married Couples with Equal Combined Income: Two married households with identical joint incomes (for instance, $200,000) pay identical federal taxes, irrespective of whether the income was earned entirely by one spouse or split equally between both spouses.
  3. Marriage Neutrality: A couple's total tax burden does not increase or decrease merely by virtue of legal marriage.

Since the Revenue Act of 1948 established the Married Filing Jointly status, Congress has chosen to prioritize the first two goals over marriage neutrality. Consequently, the tax code creates substantial windfalls (marriage bonuses) for single-earner households while imposing steep economic penalties on dual-earner couples.

Why Marriage Penalties Affect Dual High Earners While Single Earners Receive Bonuses

The fundamental driver of both marriage bonuses and marriage penalties is the ratio of earnings between spouses. According to landmark research published by the Tax Foundation and the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), tax outcomes distribute predictably along an income-split spectrum:

  • Disparate Earnings (The Marriage Bonus): When one spouse earns $140,000 and the second spouse earns $0, marriage acts as a powerful income-averaging mechanism. As unmarried singles, the primary earner faces single tax brackets topping out at 24%, with the first $15,000 protected by one standard deduction. The non-earning partner has a $15,000 single standard deduction and lower 10% and 12% bracket bands that go completely unutilized. Upon marrying, the non-working spouse's unused deduction and rate bands absorb the working spouse's income. This drops tens of thousands of dollars into lower marginal tiers, generating an annual marriage bonus often exceeding $4,000 to $6,000 in direct tax savings.
  • Equal Earnings (The Marriage Penalty): When both spouses earn substantial, roughly equal incomes (such as $150,000 + $150,000, or $275,000 + $275,000), there is no lower-earner bracket space to absorb excess income. Both individuals were already maximizing their lower rate bands and deductions as single filers. Instead, their pooled dollars rapidly trigger statutory cliffs that fail to scale to twice the single filer limits, producing an immediate marriage penalty.

Statutory Drivers of the 2026 Marriage Tax Penalty

While the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) eliminated marriage penalties across the lower and middle brackets by doubling the bracket thresholds for Married Filing Jointly across the 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, and 32% rates, four major statutory provisions continue to penalize married couples in 2026:

1. The 37% Top Marginal Bracket Threshold

Under 2026 federal bracket schedules, the 37% top rate begins at $626,350 for single filers, but at $751,600 for married couples filing jointly. If the joint threshold were double the single threshold, it would start at $1,252,700. For two professionals earning $600,000 each ($1,200,000 total household taxable income), filing as two unmarried singles keeps all income within the 35% bracket. Upon marriage, $448,400 of their income is pushed out of the 35% tier into the 37% top rate, generating an unavoidable federal regular income tax penalty of nearly $9,000 from bracket compression alone.

2. The State and Local Tax (SALT) Deduction Cap

One of the most consequential marriage penalties introduced by the TCJA is the $10,000 cap on state and local tax (SALT) deductions under Internal Revenue Code Section 164(b)(6). Crucially, the $10,000 limitation applies equally to single filers and married couples filing jointly:

Two Unmarried Individuals: $10,000 + $10,000 = $20,000 Maximum SALT Deduction
Married Filing Jointly: $10,000 Maximum Household SALT Deduction
Deduction Forfeited Upon Marriage: $10,000

In high-tax jurisdictions such as California, New York, New Jersey, and Illinois, two working professionals routinely pay more than $10,000 each in state income and real estate property taxes. When they marry, they forfeit $10,000 of legal deductions. At a 32% or 35% marginal federal tax bracket, this SALT deduction forfeiture extracts an extra $3,200 to $3,500 in federal cash out of their pockets every single year.

3. The 0.9% Additional Medicare Surtax and 3.8% NIIT Cliffs

Enacted under the Affordable Care Act, both the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on earned wages and the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) on passive capital gains and dividends feature unindexed statutory thresholds that heavily penalize dual-earner couples:

  • Single Filer Surtax Threshold: $200,000 of MAGI
  • Married Filing Jointly Threshold: $250,000 of MAGI (Instead of $400,000)

Consider two partners each earning $180,000 in salary. Unmarried, neither reaches the $200,000 threshold, resulting in $0 of Additional Medicare Tax. Upon getting married, their joint income reaches $360,000, exceeding the $250,000 threshold by $110,000. They must remit an additional $990 in Medicare surtax.

4. Child Tax Credit (CTC) Phaseouts

The Child Tax Credit provides up to $2,000 per qualifying dependent child. While the TCJA raised the phaseout beginning point to $400,000 for married couples filing jointly (exactly double the $200,000 single filer threshold), the phaseout rate of $50 per $1,000 in excess MAGI quickly eliminates the credit for dual professionals whose combined income climbs above the $400,000 boundary.

State Income Tax Marriage Penalties

In addition to federal rules, more than 15 states impose separate marriage penalties in their state tax codes. States like California, Maryland, New York, Ohio, and Wisconsin have tax brackets for married joint filers that do not double the single bracket widths across all tiers. In Maryland, for example, the 4.75% bracket tops out at $3,000 for singles and $4,500 for married filers (instead of $6,000), subjecting thousands of additional dollars to higher state tax rates upon marriage. Couples in high-tax jurisdictions must account for both federal and state bracket compressions simultaneously.

Strategic Approaches to Mitigate Marriage Penalties

Couples confronting marriage penalties should evaluate actionable tax planning measures in consultation with their financial advisors:

  1. Maximize Above-the-Line Pre-Tax Retirement Accounts: Contributing the statutory maximums to traditional 401(k), 403(b), or SEP-IRA accounts suppresses modified adjusted gross income (MAGI), potentially lowering taxable income below the Medicare surtax and bracket compression cliffs.
  2. Health Savings Accounts (HSAs): Family HSA contributions offer triple-tax-advantaged reductions to gross income, insulating up to $8,300+ from federal, state, and payroll taxation.
  3. Pass-Through Entity (PTE) Tax Workarounds: Business owners operating through S-Corporations or partnerships in over 36 states can elect to pay state income tax at the entity level, bypassing the personal $10,000 federal SALT cap limitation entirely.
  4. Evaluate Married Filing Separately (MFS) Carefully: While many taxpayers presume MFS bypasses joint income aggregation, the tax code explicitly blocks this loophole. MFS taxpayers receive half of the joint standard deduction ($15,000), a slashed $5,000 SALT cap, lose student loan interest deductions, and face stringent retirement contribution phaseouts. MFS is typically advantageous only when managing income-driven student loan repayment plans (IDR/SAVE) or isolating distinct legal liabilities.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can getting married ever increase my total take-home pay? ▼

Yes. If there is an asymmetry in your incomes (e.g., $120,000 and $20,000, or $150,000 and $0), getting married almost always increases take-home pay because the high earner's income is taxed across the wider joint brackets and absorbs the partner's unused deductions.

Why does the tax code penalize two equal high earners? ▼

Economists point to the "trilemma" of tax policy: a system cannot simultaneously maintain progressive tax rates, equal taxation of equal-earning couples, and marriage neutrality. Policymakers chose to prioritize family income pooling over marriage neutrality for high earners.

How does the timing of our wedding date impact our annual taxes? ▼

Under IRS marital status rules, your status on December 31 at 11:59 PM determines your tax status for the entire calendar year. If your scenario results in a marriage penalty, marrying on January 1 rather than December 31 preserves your unmarried single filing advantages for that prior full tax year.

How are children allocated in the unmarried single calculation? ▼

Unmarried parents cannot both claim the same dependent child. Our calculator models optimal allocation, assigning eligible dependents to the parent whose income preserves the credit against phaseouts while having sufficient tax liability to use the full $2,000 credit per child.

How does marriage impact income-driven student loan repayment plans? ▼

When married borrowers file jointly, their student loan monthly payments under plans like SAVE, PAYE, and IBR are calculated based on their combined household Adjusted Gross Income (AGI). This can dramatically inflate monthly student loan obligations. Filing as Married Filing Separately (MFS) excludes the non-borrowing spouse's income from the calculation, although it forfeits other joint tax deductions.

What are the rules in community property states? ▼

In the nine community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin), income earned by either spouse during marriage is legally deemed community income owned equally (50/50) by both spouses. Even if spouses elect to file Married Filing Separately, IRS Form 8958 is required to allocate community income and deductions equally between their two separate returns.
