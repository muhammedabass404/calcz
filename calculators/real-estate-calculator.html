Institutional Investment Underwriting Guide

The Complete Real Estate Underwriting Framework: 70% Rule, ARV & Capital Stack

A rigorous guide to valuing distressed properties, modeling financing carry costs, managing Section 1031 tax timing, and underwriting private capital.

1. The 70% Rule of House Flipping: Origin, Mechanics & Mathematics

The 70% Rule serves as the fundamental quick-filter benchmark in residential real estate redevelopment. Popularized across real estate investment networks such as BiggerPockets, the rule establishes the upper ceiling for an investor's purchase price on a distressed residential asset.

The mathematical formula for the Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO) is expressed as:

Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO) = (After Repair Value × 0.70) - Renovation Construction Budget

Novice operators often misunderstand why the discount factor is set at 30%. That 30% spread is not pure investor profit. Instead, it is divided among three primary cost pools:

  • Transaction & Disposition Friction (8% to 10%): Listing broker commissions (typically 5% to 6%), buyer agent incentives, state and municipal transfer taxes, title insurance, escrow charges, and seller-paid buyer closing concessions.
  • Financing & Carrying Overhead (5% to 7%): Short-term debt interest payments (hard money or private notes), origination points, draw administrative fees, property taxes, vacant home hazard insurance, and municipal utility bills.
  • Target Investor Net Profit Margin (13% to 15%): The essential financial return required to justify project execution risk, contractor supervision, and equity capital lockup.

When an investor purchases at or below MAO, any unforeseen cost overruns or modest market downturns erode the profit margin rather than causing a catastrophic loss of capital principal.

2. Estimating After Repair Value (ARV): Comparable Sales Methodology

The accuracy of any real estate underwriting model depends directly on the integrity of the projected After Repair Value (ARV). ARV is defined as the fair market value of a property once fully renovated to match the highest quality finish standards prevalent in its immediate micro-neighborhood.

According to research and transaction standards published by the National Association of Realtors (NAR), residential real estate valuations must be anchored in recent, arm's-length closed sales rather than speculative list prices. Professional underwriters follow strict criteria when selecting comparable properties ("comps"):

Geographic Proximity

Within 0.5 miles in urban/suburban zones. Do not cross major physical boundaries such as multi-lane highways, railroad corridors, or school district boundaries.

Recency of Sale

Closed within the preceding 90 to 180 days. In fluctuating interest rate climates, older comps fail to reflect current buyer purchasing power.

Structural Dimensionality

Gross living area within ±15% of the subject property, identical bedroom and bathroom counts, and similar building age/architectural era.

Condition and Finish Standard

The comps must represent renovated, move-in-ready properties featuring updated mechanical systems, modern kitchens, and luxury finishes.

Relying exclusively on average price-per-square-foot metrics is a dangerous practice. Price per square foot distorts reality because smaller homes inherently trade at higher unit prices due to fixed land value components, while large homes exhibit diminishing marginal returns per square foot.

3. The Holding Costs Drag: Quantifying the Silent Profit Killer

While construction costs receive the most attention, holding costs frequently make the difference between a high-yield flip and a break-even disappointment. Every additional month a property sits under construction or on the Multiple Listing Service (MLS) drains equity.

Key components of the holding cost drag include:

  • Debt Service (Interest & Carry): A $250,000 hard money loan at 11% interest generates $2,291.67 per month in pure interest overhead. A 90-day municipal permitting delay consumes $6,875 before demolition even begins.
  • Builder's Risk & Vacant Property Insurance: Standard homeowner policies do not cover vacant structures under active renovation. Specialized builder's risk insurance policies cost two to three times more than standard coverage.
  • Property Tax Accrual: Municipal real estate taxes accrue daily and are settled in full at closing, regardless of project profitability.
  • Operational Utilities & Winterization: Continuous electricity for contractor power tools, water for tile work, and gas heating during cold-weather drywall curing.

Disciplined operators model holding durations with a 30% to 50% time buffer to account for weather delays, trade contractor bottlenecks, and final building inspection scheduling.

4. Internal Revenue Code Section 1031 Like-Kind Exchanges

Real estate investors frequently seek to defer capital gains and depreciation recapture taxes through an IRC Section 1031 exchange. However, applying Section 1031 to fix-and-flip properties requires strict compliance with IRS statutory standards.

Under Section 1031, tax-deferred treatment is exclusively reserved for real property held for productive use in a trade or business, or for long-term investment. Properties acquired with the primary intent of immediate resale (fix and flips) are classified by the IRS as dealer inventory. Profits from flipping operations are taxed as ordinary business income and are subject to federal self-employment taxes (up to 15.3%), rather than favorable long-term capital gains rates.

Conversely, investors using the BRRRR strategy (Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat) who hold renovated assets in their rental portfolio for a substantial seasoning period (typically 12 to 24 months with documented tenant lease agreements) firmly establish investment intent. When subsequently disposing of such assets, they can successfully execute a 1031 exchange.

Critical statutory deadlines governing 1031 exchanges include:

45-Day Identification Window

The investor must formally identify prospective replacement properties in writing to a Qualified Intermediary (QI) within exactly 45 calendar days of closing the relinquished property sale.

180-Day Exchange Completion

The investor must complete the purchase and acquire legal title to the replacement property within 180 calendar days of closing the relinquished property (or by the tax return due date).

5. Hard Money Financing vs Conventional Mortgages: Capital Stack Economics

Securing capital for distressed property acquisitions differs substantially from standard owner-occupied home financing. Traditional commercial banks and GSE lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) require habitable property conditions with functional heating, operable plumbing, and intact roofs, automatically disqualifying most value-add deals.

Real estate redevelopers rely heavily on specialized Hard Money Lenders and private debt funds. Understanding loan terms is essential for underwriting:

  • Loan-to-Value (LTV) vs Loan-to-Cost (LTC): Lenders cap total financing at 65% to 75% of After Repair Value, or 80% to 90% of Total Project Cost (purchase price plus renovation budget).
  • Origination Points: Upfront fees of 1 to 3 points (1% to 3% of the total commitment) collected at the closing table.
  • Construction Draw Holdbacks: Renovation capital is not disbursed upfront. Lenders place rehab funds in an escrow holdback account, releasing reimbursements in tranches only after an independent inspector verifies completed work milestones.
  • Dutch Interest vs Drawn Interest: In Dutch interest structures, borrowers pay interest on the entire approved loan facility from day one. In standard structures, interest accrues only on disbursed capital plus the initial purchase advance.

6. Stress-Testing Deal Assumptions: Margin of Safety Underwriting

Professional real estate private equity firms stress-test every acquisition prior to removing contractual inspection contingencies. Three core sensitivity tests must be evaluated:

  1. Price Sensitivity (-5% and -10% ARV): If macro mortgage rate increases compress end-buyer purchasing budgets by 10%, does the project preserve invested capital or produce an unmanageable equity deficit?
  2. Renovation Cost Inflation (+15% to +20%): Structural surprises, outdated wiring, cast-iron plumbing failures, or subfloor dry rot routinely emerge during initial demolition. A dedicated 10% to 15% construction contingency is mandatory.
  3. Timeline Expansion (+60 to +90 Days): If municipal permitting or utility hookups extend the project holding period, calculate whether ongoing monthly debt service eliminates the remaining cash return.

By applying this structured underwriting approach across every potential acquisition, real estate operators maintain steady profitability across varying market cycles.

Frequently Asked Underwriting Questions

How does the 70% Rule differ in high-cost metro markets?
In high-value coastal markets where average ARVs exceed $800,000 to $1,500,000, adhering strictly to a 70% rule can make winning bids nearly impossible due to intense competition. In these environments, investors often adjust to a 75% or 80% rule. Because the gross dollar volume is elevated, a 12% to 15% net margin still yields $100,000+ in absolute profit, which adequately compensates for execution risk.
How are contractor draw schedules handled by lenders?
Hard money lenders disburse renovation funds in arrears. The investor or general contractor must self-fund each scope of work (e.g., rough plumbing and electrical rough-in). Once that milestone is completed, the borrower requests a draw inspection. The lender dispatches an inspector to confirm completion before releasing funds from the escrow holdback, typically minus an inspection fee of $150 to $300.
What is the difference between ROI and Annualized Return on Capital (ROC)?
Return on Investment (ROI) measures total net dollar profit relative to the total cash invested over the life of the project. Annualized Return on Capital adjusts that figure for time. A 20% ROI achieved over a 4-month holding period represents an annualized return velocity of 60%, whereas the same 20% ROI over an 18-month project translates to an annualized return of only 13.3%.
How does the BRRRR method allow investors to recover all invested cash?
In a successful BRRRR deal, forced appreciation creates substantial equity. For example, if an investor purchases a home for $140,000 and invests $40,000 into rehab ($180,000 total cost basis), and the resulting ARV is $250,000, a bank refinancing at 75% LTV provides a new loan of $187,500. This payoff repays the entire initial capital outlay, leaving the investor with an income-generating rental property and infinite cash-on-cash return.
What contingency reserve percentage should be included in a renovation budget?
Industry best practices dictate maintaining a minimum 10% to 15% contingency reserve above written general contractor bids for cosmetic renovations, and 20% for historic properties or complete gut rehabilitations where concealed foundation, plumbing, and structural framing defects are common.
