Rules of Probability Cheat Sheet

Formal mathematical axioms, theorems, and identities governing probability theory, derived from the Andrey Kolmogorov axiomatic formulation (1933).

1

Kolmogorov Axioms (1933)

Three foundational axioms establishing a rigorous measure-theoretic basis:

  • Axiom 1 (Non-negativity):
    $P(E) \ge 0 \quad \forall E \in \mathcal{F}$
  • Axiom 2 (Unitarity):
    $P(\Omega) = 1$
  • Axiom 3 ($\sigma$-Additivity):
    $P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty E_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty P(E_i)$
    For pairwise disjoint events
2

Addition Theorems

Probability that event A, event B, or both occur:

  • General Addition Rule:
    $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$
  • Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint):
    $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$
    Since $P(A \cap B) = 0$
  • Three-Event Inclusion-Exclusion:
    $P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C)$
3

Multiplication Theorems

Joint occurrence of multiple events:

  • General Multiplication Rule:
    $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A) = P(B) \cdot P(A|B)$
  • Statistical Independence:
    $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B) \iff P(A|B) = P(A)$
  • Chain Rule ($n$ Events):
    $P\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i \right) = P(A_1) \prod_{i=2}^n P(A_i | \bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1} A_j)$
4

Conditioning & Total Probability

Evaluating probabilities under partial information:

  • Conditional Probability:
    $P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \quad (P(B) > 0)$
  • Law of Total Probability:
    $P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^k P(A|B_i) P(B_i)$
    For partition $B_1, \dots, B_k$ of $\Omega$
5

Bayes' Theorem

Inverting conditional probabilities:

  • Standard Posterior Form:
    $P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A) P(A)}{P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|A')P(A')}$
  • Odds Formulation:
    $\text{Posterior Odds} = \text{Prior Odds} \times \frac{P(B|A)}{P(B|A')}$
6

Complements & De Morgan

Set theoretic probability dualities:

  • Complement Rule:
    $P(A') = 1 - P(A)$
  • De Morgan's Laws:
    $P((A \cup B)') = P(A' \cap B')$
    $P((A \cap B)') = P(A' \cup B')$
  • At Least Once Rule:
    $P(\text{at least 1 in } n) = 1 - (1 - p)^n$

Foundations of Probability Theory: Kolmogorov Axioms, Bayesian Inference, and Decision Science

A rigorous exploration of mathematical measure theory, conditional dependence structures, machine learning classifiers, and clinical screening paradigms.

1. The Axiomatic Foundation: Andrey Kolmogorov (1933)

Probability theory developed over centuries through empirical gambling puzzles posed to Gerolamo Cardano, Blaise Pascal, and Pierre de Fermat. However, early definitions based solely on classical ratios of "equally likely outcomes" suffered from logical circularity, while frequentist limits lacked foundational stability over continuous infinite spaces. This theoretical deficiency was resolved in 1933 when Soviet mathematician Andrey Nikolaevich Kolmogorov published his landmark monograph, Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung (Foundations of the Theory of Probability).

Kolmogorov established probability within the rigorous mathematical framework of measure theory. A formal probability space is defined as a triplet $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$:

  • Sample Space ($\Omega$): The universal set containing all conceivable elementary outcomes $\omega$.
  • Event Space ($\mathcal{F}$): A $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$. By definition, $\mathcal{F}$ contains the empty set $\emptyset$, is closed under complementation ($A \in \mathcal{F} \implies A' \in \mathcal{F}$), and is closed under countable unions ($A_i \in \mathcal{F} \implies \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i \in \mathcal{F}$).
  • Probability Measure ($P$): A real-valued function assigning a measure from $\mathcal{F}$ onto the closed interval $[0, 1]$, governed by three fundamental axioms.

Axiom 1 (Non-Negativity): For every event $E \in \mathcal{F}$, $P(E) \ge 0$. Probability values are non-negative real numbers.

Axiom 2 (Unitarity): $P(\Omega) = 1$. The probability that some outcome in the sample space occurs equals certainty.

Axiom 3 (Countable Additivity): For any infinite sequence of mutually disjoint events $E_1, E_2, E_3, \dots$ (where $E_i \cap E_j = \emptyset$ for all $i eq j$):

$$P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty E_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty P(E_i)$$

From these three axioms, all fundamental theorems of classical probability follow deductively. The complement rule $P(A') = 1 - P(A)$ is derived by decomposing $\Omega = A \cup A'$, giving $P(\Omega) = P(A) + P(A') = 1$. Similarly, because the empty set is the complement of $\Omega$, its probability is strictly zero: $P(\emptyset) = 0$.

2. Addition and Multiplication Rules: Disjointness versus Independence

A frequent source of analytical confusion involves conflating mutually exclusive (disjoint) events with statistically independent events. These concepts represent entirely distinct structural relationships.

Two events $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive when they cannot occur concurrently. In set theory terms, their intersection is the null set: $A \cap B = \emptyset$, meaning $P(A \cap B) = 0$. Under this condition, the addition rule simplifies directly to:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

When events are not mutually exclusive, adding their individual probabilities double-counts the region where both occur simultaneously. The general addition rule resolves this overlap via subtraction:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

In contrast, two events are statistically independent when the occurrence of one provides zero information regarding the likelihood of the other. Mathematically, independence is defined by the product rule:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$$

This definition reveals a fundamental mathematical truth: two non-trivial events ($P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$) that are mutually exclusive can never be independent. If $A$ occurs, the probability of $B$ immediately collapses to zero: $P(B|A) = 0 eq P(B)$. Knowing that $A$ occurred provides total information about $B$.

3. Bayes' Theorem in Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence

Formulated by English statistician Thomas Bayes and independently expanded by Pierre-Simon Laplace, Bayes' theorem provides a systematic method for updating subjective or empirical probabilities in light of new evidence. The standard formula states:

$$P(H|E) = \frac{P(E|H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E)}$$

In machine learning pipelines, Bayes' rule underpins classification architectures, natural language processing models, and reinforcement learning state estimators. Consider a classification problem where an algorithm seeks to assign an input feature vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d)$ to one of $K$ discrete classes $C_k$:

  • Prior Probability $P(C_k)$: The baseline frequency of class $C_k$ in the training corpus.
  • Likelihood $P(\mathbf{x}|C_k)$: The probability density of observing feature vector $\mathbf{x}$ given that the true class is $C_k$.
  • Posterior Probability $P(C_k|\mathbf{x})$: The updated conditional probability that the observation belongs to class $C_k$ after evaluating feature vector $\mathbf{x}$.

In high-dimensional feature spaces, evaluating full joint likelihood distributions $P(x_1, \dots, x_d | C_k)$ becomes computationally intractable. The Naive Bayes Classifier solves this bottleneck by introducing a conditional independence assumption: each individual feature $x_i$ is assumed conditionally independent of every other feature $x_j$ given the class label $C_k$. This simplifies the likelihood calculation into an efficient product of one-dimensional densities:

$$P(C_k | \mathbf{x}) \propto P(C_k) \prod_{i=1}^d P(x_i | C_k)$$

Despite the obvious violation of feature independence in human language (where word sequences exhibit strong syntactic correlations), Naive Bayes classifiers provide exceptional accuracy and runtime speed for automated spam detection, sentiment analysis, and medical symptom screening.

4. Clinical Diagnostics and the Base Rate Fallacy: A Worked Case Study

In clinical medicine, failing to integrate prior disease prevalence into diagnostic interpretations leads directly to the base rate fallacy (base rate neglect). Diagnostic tests are typically characterized by two laboratory parameters:

Sensitivity (True Positive Rate): The conditional probability that the diagnostic test returns positive given that the patient truly has the disease: $P(+|D)$.
Specificity (True Negative Rate): The conditional probability that the test returns negative given that the patient is healthy: $P(-|D')$.

Clinicians frequently commit the error of equating test sensitivity with predictive accuracy. To observe why this intuition fails, examine the following worked mathematical scenario:

Worked Case: Screening 100,000 Asymptomatic Patients

  • Disease Prevalence: $P(D) = 0.2\%$ ($2$ in $1,000$ individuals).
  • Test Sensitivity: $99.0\%$ ($0.99$).
  • Test Specificity: $95.0\%$ ($0.95$), yielding a False Positive Rate of $5.0\%$ ($0.05$).

Step 1: Cohort Segmentation

In a cohort of $100,000$ individuals, exactly $200$ have the disease, while $99,800$ are disease-free.

Step 2: Diseased Group Testing ($200$ people)

True Positives ($TP$) = $200 \times 0.99 = 198$ individuals.

False Negatives ($FN$) = $200 \times 0.01 = 2$ individuals.

Step 3: Healthy Group Testing ($99,800$ people)

False Positives ($FP$) = $99,800 \times 0.05 = 4,990$ individuals.

True Negatives ($TN$) = $99,800 \times 0.95 = 94,810$ individuals.

Step 4: Evaluating Positive Predictive Value (PPV)

The total number of positive test results is $198 + 4,990 = 5,188$. Applying Bayes' formula:

$$P(D|+) = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} = \frac{198}{5,188} \approx 3.82\%$$

Despite a test exhibiting $99\%$ sensitivity and $95\%$ specificity, an asymptomatic patient who tests positive possesses only a $3.82\%$ chance of actually having the disease. Over $96\%$ of all positive test results represent false alarms. Because the disease is rare, the $5\%$ error rate acting upon the massive healthy subpopulation ($99,800$ people) produces far more false positives ($4,990$) than the test produces true positives ($198$) from the small infected cohort.

5. Odds Ratios, Bayes Factors, and Decision Science

While probability measures the ratio of favorable events to total possible events $P = a / (a + b)$, the concept of odds represents the direct ratio of favorable outcomes to unfavorable outcomes:

$$\text{Odds in Favor} = \frac{P}{1 - P} = \frac{a}{b}, \quad P = \frac{\text{Odds}}{1 + \text{Odds}}$$

In Bayesian decision theory, transforming probabilities into odds simplifies sequential inference into a linear update rule. Expressing Bayes' theorem in odds formulation yields:

$$\text{Posterior Odds} = \text{Prior Odds} \times \text{Bayes Factor}$$

The Bayes Factor (or likelihood ratio $\frac{P(E|H)}{P(E|H')}$) quantifies how strongly the observed data supports the hypothesis relative to its alternative. Taking the logarithm of both sides transforms belief revision into an additive accumulation of evidence:

$$\log(\text{Posterior Odds}) = \log(\text{Prior Odds}) + \log(\text{Bayes Factor})$$

This logarithmic formulation forms the mathematical bridge connecting Bayesian probability updates to information theory (Shannon bits of evidence) and modern logistic regression algorithms.
