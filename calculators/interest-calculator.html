The Mathematics of Interest: Simple Accrual vs. Compound Growth and Rate Conversions

Interest represents the rental cost of capital. For lenders and investors, interest provides compensation for deferred consumption, opportunity cost, inflation erosion, and default risk. For borrowers, interest is the premium paid to access liquidity today rather than in the future. The two fundamental mathematical mechanisms governing interest accrual are simple interest (linear growth) and compound interest (exponential accumulation). Selecting the proper accrual model is critical for evaluating savings accounts, bonds, certificates of deposit (CDs), and debt obligations.

Simple Interest: Definitions and Formulas

Simple interest is calculated solely on the original principal sum. Accrued interest is not added to the principal base for subsequent calculations:

I = P × r × t

Where I is total interest earned, P is initial principal, r is annual interest rate, and t is time duration in years. The total ending amount is:

A = P(1 + rt)

Simple interest is commonly used in short-term promissory notes, auto dealer financing promotions, and municipal bond coupon accruals.

Compound Interest and Frequency Mechanics

Compound interest arises when interest earned over a cycle is credited to the principal, so subsequent interest is calculated on both initial principal and accumulated interest:

A = P \left(1 + \frac{r}{n} \right)^{nt}

When compounding occurs continuously (as the interval approaches zero), the equation resolves to Euler's exponential constant:

A = P \cdot e^{rt}

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Comparison: $20,000 over 10 Years at 7.0%

Case A: Simple Interest
I = $20,000 × 0.07 × 10 = $14,000.00.
Ending Balance = $20,000 + $14,000 = $34,000.00.

Case B: Annual Compounding (n = 1)
A = $20,000 × (1.07)^{10} = $20,000 × 1.967151 = $39,343.03 (+$5,343 over simple!).

Case C: Monthly Compounding (n = 12)
A = $20,000 × (1 + 0.07/12)^{120} = $20,000 × (1.0058333)^{120} = $20,000 × 2.009661 = $40,193.23 (+$6,193 over simple!).

Case D: Continuous Compounding
A = $20,000 × e^{0.07 × 10} = $20,000 × e^{0.70} = $20,000 × 2.013753 = $40,275.05.

Annual Percentage Rate (APR) vs. Annual Percentage Yield (APY)

Under the federal Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and Truth in Savings Act (TISA), lenders and depository banks are legally obligated to publish specific interest disclosures:

Metric Primary Context Compounding Effect Included Mathematical Formula
APR (Annual Percentage Rate) Loans, credit cards, mortgages No (simple annualized interest + upfront fees) r × n
APY (Annual Percentage Yield) Savings accounts, money markets, CDs Yes (reflects true annual compound growth) (1 + r/n)^{n} - 1

Frequently Asked Questions

How do credit cards calculate monthly interest?

Credit card issuers compute interest using the Average Daily Balance (ADB) method. Your APR is divided by 365 to derive a Daily Periodic Rate (DPR). The DPR is multiplied by each day's closing balance and summed across billing cycle days.

Why does compounding frequency offer diminishing returns at higher intervals?

Moving from annual to monthly compounding increases yield noticeably. However, moving from daily compounding (365 cycles) to hourly or continuous compounding adds only tiny fractions of a cent, as defined by the mathematical limit lim(n→∞)(1 + 1/n)^{n} = e ≈ 2.71828.

How are interest earnings taxed?

Interest from standard high-yield savings accounts, CDs, and corporate bonds is taxed at ordinary federal income tax rates (up to 37%), not lower long-term capital gains rates. US Treasury bond interest is exempt from state and local income taxes.

What is the difference between real and nominal interest rates?

Nominal interest is the stated dollar return. The real interest rate adjusts for inflation: Real Rate = Nominal Rate - Inflation Rate. If a savings account pays 5% APY while inflation runs at 3%, your real purchasing power expands by 2%.
